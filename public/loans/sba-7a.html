SBA 7(a) Loan

The most flexible SBA loan, for almost any business purpose.

Updated November 2, 2026.

What it is

The SBA 7(a) is the Small Business Administration's workhorse loan. A bank or other approved lender makes the loan, and the SBA guarantees a large share of it. That guarantee is what lets lenders say yes to businesses that might not qualify for a conventional loan, usually with longer terms and smaller down payments.

Because one program covers so many uses (working capital, equipment, real estate, buying a business, and refinancing certain business debt), it is the first loan most small business owners should price out, even if they end up choosing another product.

Source: SBA Information Notice 5000-881797. Manufacturers, certain food-supply-chain businesses and rural businesses pay 0% on loans of $700,000 or less.

The guarantee protects the lender if the loan goes bad. You still owe the full balance.

A good fit if you want

  • Buying or starting a business
  • Longer repayment terms that keep monthly payments lower
  • Businesses that fall just short of conventional lending standards
  • Mixed-use needs in one loan: equipment, working capital and real estate

Probably not the right tool if

  • Very small amounts, where a microloan or credit line may be simpler
  • Borrowers who need money in days, not weeks
  • Passive real-estate investing or other ineligible business types

Typical requirements

  • For-profit business operating in the U.S. that meets SBA size standards
  • Owners with 20% or more typically sign a personal guarantee
  • Owner equity in the business and a clear repayment story (see DSCR)
  • Unable to get credit on reasonable terms elsewhere (the "credit elsewhere" test)
  • Current on taxes and any federal debt

Maximum interest rates

On variable-rate 7(a) loans the lender may charge the base rate plus a spread, and the SBA caps that spread by loan size (SBA terms and conditions). The current prime rate is 7.00% (as of November 6, 2026; source: FRED DPRIME). Your actual rate is negotiated and is often lower than the cap.

Worked example: a $350,000 loan, all-in

Illustration as of November 2026, using the FY 2027 fee schedule (SBA Information Notice 5000-881797) and a prime rate of 7.00% (FRED DPRIME, October 27, 2026). Not an offer; your rate and fees will differ.

  • Loan: $350,000, 10-year term, variable rate assumed at 10.00% (prime + 3.00%, below the 11.50% cap for this size).
  • SBA guarantee fee: the SBA guarantees 75% of a loan this size, or $262,500. At the 3% tier that is a fee of $7,875. The lender pays the SBA and normally passes it to you, either in cash at closing or financed into the loan.
  • Monthly payment: about $4,625 on $350,000 (about $4,729 if the fee is financed).
  • All-in cost over 10 years, fee paid in cash: about $205,033 interest + $7,875 fee = about $212,908 on top of the $350,000 principal.

Amortization

In year one about $34,034 of the payments is interest and about $21,470 reduces principal. After 5 years the balance is about $217,690. Early payments are interest-heavy, which is why extra principal payments help most in the first years. The calculator above shows the full schedule.

DSCR check

Annual debt service is about $55,503 ($4,625 × 12). Lenders commonly look for a debt service coverage ratio of at least 1.25, which here means net operating income of about $69,400 or more. A business with $85,000 of net operating income would show a DSCR of about 1.53. See DSCR explained for how lenders calculate it.

How to apply

  1. Check eligibilityConfirm your business type, size standard and owner requirements before spending time on paperwork.
  2. Find a lenderStart with your own bank. If you need help finding one, SBA Lender Match is a free matching service that passes your request to participating lenders; it is not an approval. Separately, a Preferred Lender (PLP) is a lender the SBA has delegated authority to, so it can make the credit decision itself without sending the file to the SBA.
  3. Gather documentsPlan on SBA Form 1919, a personal financial statement (Form 413), 3 years of business and personal tax returns, current financial statements, a debt schedule and a business plan or projections for new ventures.
  4. Submit and underwriteThe lender reviews credit, cash flow and collateral and may ask follow-up questions. Fast responses keep the file moving.
  5. ApprovalIf the lender does not have delegated authority, the SBA reviews the file before approval, which adds time. Review times vary by lender, loan size and how complete the package is, and the SBA does not guarantee a turnaround, so ask your lender for its own estimate.
  6. Close and fundReview the final terms, sign, and fund. Closing time depends on the lender, the collateral and third-party reports (appraisal, environmental, title), so ask for a timeline in writing; weeks rather than days is a reasonable planning assumption, not an SBA-published figure.

Need a lender, an SBA district office or a free counselor? Use the national directory.

Common questions

Is an SBA loan from the government?

No. The lender makes the loan; the SBA guarantees part of it to reduce the lender's risk.

Who pays the SBA fee?

The lender pays the upfront guaranty fee to the SBA and may pass it on to the borrower. The lender's annual service fee cannot be passed on. Manufacturers, certain food supply chain businesses and rural businesses can pay 0% upfront on loans of $700,000 or less.

Is there a prepayment penalty?

For loans with maturities of 15 years or more, paying off 25% or more of the balance in the first three years carries a fee of 5%, 3% and 1% of the amount prepaid in years one, two and three.

Fixed or variable rate?

Either. Variable-rate loans are priced off a base rate such as prime plus a spread that cannot exceed the SBA maximum for the loan size.
