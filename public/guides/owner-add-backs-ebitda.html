Owner Add-Backs and EBITDA Normalization

Short answer: Normalization adjusts reported profit for items that are not part of ongoing operations, such as owner pay above market, personal expenses, one-time costs, interest and depreciation, to show what a new owner or lender can expect the business to earn. Each add-back needs documentation that ties to the tax returns and books, and lenders accept only what they can verify.

If you are buying or selling an owner-operated business, or borrowing against one, the number everyone argues about is not net income. It is adjusted earnings. This guide explains how the adjustments work, how they flow into a lender’s coverage test, and where they go wrong. Figures are illustrations as of November 11, 2026.

EBITDA vs. SDE vs. net income

  • EBITDA: net income plus interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization. It measures operating earnings before financing and tax choices.
  • Adjusted (normalized) EBITDA: EBITDA after add-backs for non-operating and one-time items, and after replacing the owner’s pay with what a manager would cost.
  • Seller’s discretionary earnings (SDE): earnings before one owner’s compensation and benefits, also adjusted. Brokers use SDE for smaller businesses where the owner works in the business. The business acquisition calculator starts from SDE and subtracts a market salary for the operator, because a lender will want the new owner paid.

At a 2.8x SDE multiple that business is priced near $907,000. A lender, though, will test repayment from a cash-flow figure it can verify. Suppose the annual debt service on the acquisition is about $120,921 (the starting case in the acquisition calculator). Adjusted EBITDA of $244,000 gives a coverage ratio of about 2.02x. If the lender rejects the $15,000 settlement and the $9,000 of vehicle costs as unsupported, the figure is $220,000 and the ratio is about 1.82x. Each $10,000 of rejected add-backs moves this ratio by about 0.08x, and on a thinner deal that is the difference between approval and a decline.

Common add-backs, and the proof lenders want

  • Excess owner compensation. Compare actual W-2 pay and benefits with the cost to hire someone for the same role. For an S corporation, owner-employees must also receive reasonable compensation (IRS: S corporation compensation), so the add-back cannot take pay below a defensible wage.
  • Personal expenses. Vehicles, travel, meals, family cell phones. Needs ledger detail and receipts. Expect scrutiny of anything that also looks like a normal operating cost.
  • One-time or non-recurring costs. Litigation, a move, a failed project. Needs the document that proves it happened once.
  • Non-cash items. Depreciation and amortization. These are routine, but replacement capital spending is still real; a buyer will ask what equipment must be replaced.
  • Related-party rent or loans. Restate to market rent and remove related-party interest if it ends at closing.

Add-backs that usually fail

  • Cash revenue not in the books or on the return (the lender cannot count what was never reported)
  • Recurring costs relabeled as one-time
  • A salary add-back that leaves the operator unpaid
  • Projected savings or growth presented as historical earnings

Multi-state and pass-through details

For multi-state operators, confirm payroll and state tax filings are consistent before adding anything back; unfiled states are a diligence problem, not an add-back. S corporation distributions are not compensation, so a low W-2 with large distributions can overstate normalized earnings once a reasonable wage is applied. Flow-through income also means the owner’s tax is paid personally, which matters in a global coverage test.

How this feeds the loan

SBA lenders decide repayment ability from historical financial statements and tax returns, so a clean, documented set of add-backs matters as much as the number itself. Pair this guide with the documents lenders ask for, the cash-to-close estimator, and the SBA 7(a) page for terms (see SBA 7(a) loans).

Checklist

  1. Start from the filed returns for the last three years.
  2. List every add-back with the document that supports it.
  3. Replace owner pay with a market wage and note the source.
  4. Show replacement capital spending separately.
  5. Reconcile adjusted earnings to bank deposits.

Common questions

What is the difference between EBITDA and SDE?

EBITDA excludes interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization. SDE also adds back one owner’s pay and benefits, and is common for owner-operated businesses.

Do lenders accept all add-backs?

No. Lenders generally accept adjustments they can tie to tax returns, financial statements and other documents, and may reject the rest.

Should I replace owner pay when normalizing earnings?

Yes. A lender expects the operator to be paid, so adjusted earnings are usually shown after a market wage for the role.

Can cash revenue be added back?

Generally no. Lenders cannot count income that is not in the books and on the tax return.
