SBA Loan Eligibility: Who Qualifies

Short answer: To qualify for an SBA loan, a business generally must be a for-profit U.S. business that meets SBA size standards, have owners who invest equity and sign a personal guarantee, and be unable to get credit elsewhere on reasonable terms.

SBA-backed loans have a reputation for being hard to get, but most rejections come from a handful of predictable eligibility problems rather than from the paperwork. Knowing those rules before you apply saves weeks. This guide walks through the tests a lender will run on your business, your owners, and your reason for borrowing.

1. The business must be an eligible type

The basic requirement is that you operate as a for-profit business that does business in the United States or its territories. Sole proprietorships, partnerships, LLCs, and corporations can all qualify. Within that, the SBA excludes certain activities. Common examples of ineligible businesses include:

  • Businesses that are primarily passive, such as those that hold real estate or investments only to collect rent or appreciation.
  • Lending and investment businesses, and many types of financial intermediaries.
  • Businesses engaged in illegal activity under federal law.
  • Pyramid and multi-level distribution plans.
  • Gambling businesses beyond narrow limits on incidental revenue.
  • Non-profit organizations and government-owned entities.

The list is longer and has details; the SBA's standard operating procedures are the authority, and your lender will confirm it in the first meeting.

2. The business must meet the SBA size standard

The program exists for small businesses, and "small" is defined by industry. The SBA publishes size standards by NAICS code, expressed as either average annual revenue or number of employees. A restaurant, a trucking company, and a software firm all have different thresholds. There is also an alternative size standard based on tangible net worth and average net income that some borrowers can use if they exceed their industry standard. Look up your NAICS code and your threshold before you apply, and remember affiliated businesses are usually counted together.

3. Owners must have real equity at stake

The SBA expects borrowers to put in their own money. For a startup or an acquisition, lenders commonly look for an owner contribution (often discussed as roughly 10 percent of the project, though it varies by lender and deal). For established businesses, they look at whether the owners have invested in the company and whether they are willing to stand behind the loan. This is not a fee; it is evidence that the borrower shares the risk.

4. Owners generally must guarantee the loan

Anyone who owns 20 percent or more of the business is typically required to provide an unlimited personal guarantee. If the business cannot repay, the lender can pursue those owners personally. Smaller owners may also be asked to guarantee, depending on the lender's policy. Spouses of owners are sometimes asked to sign documents that relate to collateral. Make sure every owner understands this before the application starts.

5. You must show you cannot get the credit on reasonable terms elsewhere

The "credit elsewhere" test means the SBA program is meant for borrowers who cannot obtain the funds from other sources on reasonable terms. In practice the lender documents why a conventional loan is not available to you, for example a shorter term, a larger down payment, or tighter collateral requirements. The test is less strict than the name suggests, but you should expect to be asked about it, and your answers should be honest and consistent.

6. The owners must be in good standing

The lender will run credit checks and ask about prior defaults. Delinquent federal debt, including past SBA loans, student loans, and taxes, can stop an application until it is cured or under a repayment arrangement. Pending criminal charges, probation, or parole can also affect eligibility. Disclose everything on the personal history forms; omissions are a bigger problem than the history itself. Rules about owner citizenship and residency have been updated in recent years, so confirm the current requirements with your lender.

7. The loan must be for an eligible purpose

Typical eligible uses include working capital, equipment, buying or building real estate you will occupy, acquiring an existing business, and refinancing certain business debt. Ineligible uses include paying delinquent payroll taxes in some cases, funding distributions to owners, and speculation. Be specific in your application about what each dollar will do.

A quick self-check

  • Is my business for-profit and operating in the U.S.?
  • Is my industry on the ineligible list?
  • Do I meet the size standard for my NAICS code?
  • Can I show real owner equity and a plan for repayment?
  • Are all 20 percent owners prepared to sign personal guarantees?
  • Are my taxes, existing loans, and government debts current?

If you can answer yes to these, you are in a strong position. If you cannot, talk to a local SBA resource partner. Counselors at SCORE and Small Business Development Centers offer free advising, and you can find nearby offices in our national directory.
