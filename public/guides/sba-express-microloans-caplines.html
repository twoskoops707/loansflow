SBA Express, Microloans, and CAPLines: The Smaller SBA Options

When people say "SBA loan" they usually mean the standard 7(a) or the 504 real estate program. But the SBA backs several smaller or more specialized products that fit situations those two do not: a $30,000 equipment purchase, a seasonal inventory build, or a first loan for a business that banks consider too small. This guide explains three of the most useful ones, SBA Express, SBA Microloans, and CAPLines, and how to decide whether one of them beats the standard 7(a).

SBA Express loans

Express is a version of the 7(a) program built for speed. The lender uses its own forms and much of its own underwriting process instead of the full SBA package, and the SBA commits to responding to an Express request quickly. In exchange for that simplicity, the SBA guarantees a smaller share of the loan, 50 percent, compared with 75 to 85 percent on a standard 7(a). The maximum Express loan is $500,000.

A smaller guarantee means the lender carries more risk, so Express lenders tend to be stricter on collateral and cash flow than their marketing suggests. The practical advantage is turnaround: a lender that already knows your deposit history can often move faster on an Express than on a full 7(a). Express can also be structured as a revolving line of credit, which a standard term loan cannot do.

When Express makes sense

  • You need under $500,000 and a decision in days rather than weeks.
  • You already bank with an SBA lender that has Express authority.
  • You want a revolving line of credit with SBA-backed terms.

When it does not

  • You need more than $500,000, in which case the standard 7(a) or the 504 program is the right tool.
  • You have weak collateral. A 50 percent guarantee gives the lender less cushion to approve a marginal file.

SBA Microloans

Microloans work differently from every other SBA product. The SBA does not guarantee a bank loan; it lends money to nonprofit community organizations (called intermediaries), which then make the loans to small businesses. The maximum is $50,000, and the intermediary sets many of the terms within SBA rules. Repayment terms are limited to six years.

Microloan proceeds can fund working capital, inventory, supplies, furniture, fixtures, and equipment. They cannot be used to buy real estate or to pay off existing debt. That limit matters: if your goal is refinancing, a microloan is the wrong product.

The real value of a microloan is usually the intermediary itself. Many pair the loan with business training, mentoring, or technical help, and they are far more willing than a bank to work with startups, newer owners, and borrowers rebuilding credit. Expect to be asked for a business plan, cash-flow projections, and a clear explanation of how the loan will be repaid, but expect a more personal conversation than at a large bank. Use the SBA's lender match and intermediary search to find an organization that serves your area.

CAPLines

CAPLines is the SBA's family of revolving working-capital programs. Rather than a lump sum, you get a line you draw on as the business needs it. There are four types, each designed for a specific cash-flow problem:

  • Seasonal: finances the buildup of inventory, labor, and receivables before a predictable busy season.
  • Contract: covers the direct costs of performing a specific contract or purchase order, such as labor and materials.
  • Builders: supports the direct costs of construction or renovation for a contractor or builder.
  • Working capital: a general revolving line secured by receivables and inventory, usually with ongoing reporting.

CAPLines carry the same $5 million maximum as the 7(a) program. Maturities are capped at 10 years, except Builders lines, which are limited to 60 months plus the time needed to finish construction. Because the collateral is often receivables and inventory that change constantly, lenders typically require regular borrowing-base reporting. That administrative load is the main trade-off: it is more work than a plain term loan, but the line matches how cash actually moves through the business.

How to choose

Start from the use of proceeds, not the program name:

  1. Real estate or a major equipment purchase over a few hundred thousand dollars: look at the 504 program or a standard 7(a) loan.
  2. Under $50,000 and early-stage: start with a microloan intermediary.
  3. Up to $500,000 and speed matters: ask your bank about 7(a) Express.
  4. Cash flow that swings with seasons or contracts: ask about the CAPLines type that matches.

Whichever you choose, confirm you meet the basics first. Our eligibility guide covers the size standards, owner equity, and the "credit elsewhere" requirement that apply across these programs, and the document checklist will help you gather what the lender asks for.

Questions to ask any SBA lender

  • Are you a Preferred Lender, and do you have Express authority?
  • What is the all-in cost, including the guaranty fee and any packaging or closing fees?
  • What collateral do you require, and will you lien my home?
  • How long from complete application to funding on a deal like mine?
