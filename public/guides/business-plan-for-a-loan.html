The Business Plan and Financial Projections Lenders Want to See

Many lenders will tell you that a loan application is a story backed by numbers. The business plan is where you tell the story, and the financial projections are the proof. A good plan is not a long one. It is a clear, specific document that lets a stranger understand what you do, why it works, and how the loan gets repaid.

Who needs a full plan

A startup, an acquisition, or a major expansion almost always needs a complete plan. An established business with several years of tax returns may be asked for a shorter narrative focused on the loan purpose. In both cases the lender cares most about repayment, so build everything around that question.

The sections that matter

1. Executive summary

One page, written last. State what the business does, how much you are requesting, what it will be used for, and why you expect to repay it. If a reader stopped here, they should know whether the deal is credible.

2. Company description and ownership

Legal structure, location, history, and who owns what. Lenders need to know every owner of 20 percent or more because those owners usually guarantee the loan.

3. Products, services, and customers

What you sell, to whom, and why they pick you. Name your customer types and, if possible, your largest customers and how concentrated your revenue is. Heavy dependence on one customer is a risk the lender will ask about.

4. Market and competition

Describe your local or niche market using real sources, such as census data, trade associations, and industry reports, and be honest about competitors. A plan that claims no competition is not credible.

5. Management and staffing

Short biographies showing relevant experience. Lenders weigh management heavily, especially for startups and acquisitions. If there is a gap, explain how you will fill it with an advisor, a hire, or training.

6. Marketing and operations

How you find customers, how you deliver, who your suppliers are, and what systems keep it running. Keep it concrete: channels, prices, and costs.

7. Use of funds (sources and uses)

A table listing every dollar of the project and where it comes from. For example, equipment, working capital, and closing costs on the uses side, and the loan, your cash contribution, and seller financing on the sources side. The two sides must match exactly. Lenders pay close attention to this table.

Building financial projections

Projections should connect to history and be explainable. A reasonable package contains:

  • Monthly projections for the first 12 months, then annual figures for years two and three: income statement, cash flow, and balance sheet.
  • A written list of assumptions. Price, volume, growth rate, gross margin, payroll, rent, and other major costs, each with the reason you chose it.
  • A break-even analysis showing the monthly revenue you need to cover fixed costs.
  • Debt service shown explicitly, with the resulting DSCR in each year.

Make the assumptions defensible

Anchor projections to something verifiable: past sales, signed contracts, industry benchmark margins, or quotes from vendors. Avoid straight-line "10 percent growth every year" with no explanation. Many lenders also want a conservative scenario in which sales come in 15 to 20 percent below plan, and the loan still can be repaid.

Check your numbers against your history

If you have operated for a while, projections should not contradict your tax returns and financial statements without a good reason. A sudden jump in margin needs an explanation, such as a new product line, a price increase, or reduced cost of goods.

Attachments that support the plan

  • Resumes of owners and key managers
  • Business and personal tax returns for the last three years
  • Current profit-and-loss statement and balance sheet
  • Lease, purchase agreement, quotes, or contracts
  • Licenses and permits

Mistakes that sink applications

  • A repayment story that depends on the loan itself producing a miracle, with no evidence behind it.
  • Sources and uses that do not add up.
  • Projections that never show a bad month.
  • Hiding or glossing over weaknesses, such as a past tax lien or a customer you just lost.
  • Long, generic text copied from a template with no specifics about your business.

Free help

SCORE mentors and Small Business Development Centers will review your plan and projections at no cost, and many lenders are happier to see a file that an advisor has looked at. Find nearby offices in the national directory, then follow the process in the SBA 7(a) playbook.
