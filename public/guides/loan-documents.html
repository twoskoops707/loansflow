Loan documents: what lenders ask for, by loan type

Lenders across every loan category ask for the same core set of documents, then add a layer of program-specific forms on top. Understanding that structure lets you build one master file instead of scrambling at closing. Every checklist below is based on publicly available lender guides and SBA instructions; your lender's exact list controls, but the items below cover what you will almost certainly need.

Behind those requests are the five C's of credit — character, capacity, capital, collateral, and conditions — that every lender evaluates before approving a loan. Character is your track record: credit history, business resumes, and management experience all signal whether you will repay. Capacity is your ability to service the debt, measured through cash flow, DSCR, and income documentation. Capital is the skin in the game you have already invested or are putting in — down payment, retained earnings, and personal funds. Collateral is the asset the lender can seize if you default, which ranges from equipment and receivables to real estate and inventory. Conditions cover the purpose of the loan, the state of your industry, and the broader economic climate. Together these five factors explain why lenders ask for the same information in slightly different forms. If you want to see how the numbers side of capacity works, see DSCR explained.

The core package

Almost every business loan application starts with the same stack of documents. Having these ready before you apply cuts weeks off the timeline and signals organization to the underwriter.

  • Government-issued photo ID — driver's license, passport, or state ID for each owner.
  • Social Security numbers for every owner with 20 percent or more of the business.
  • EIN confirmation letter — IRS CP 575, 147C, or a recent IRS 627 letter verifying the business's employer identification number.
  • Formation documents — articles of incorporation, articles of organization, or equivalent, plus any bylaws, operating agreement, or partnership agreement.
  • Business licenses and permits — state, county, and city-level licenses required for your industry.
  • DBA registration if you operate under a fictitious business name.
  • Three years of business federal tax returns — all schedules, including Schedule C, K-1, or 1120 as applicable.
  • Three years of personal federal tax returns for every owner with 20 percent or more stake, again with all schedules.
  • Year-to-date profit and loss statement dated within the last 90 days, plus a current balance sheet.
  • Twelve months of business bank statements — all business accounts.
  • Debt schedule listing every existing loan, line of credit, and lease obligation with balances, rates, and maturity dates.
  • Business overview or history — one to three pages explaining what the company does, its major customers or clients, and its current operational status.
  • Resumes of principals — one page each, emphasizing management experience and industry background.

If you run a franchise, include the franchise agreement. If you are buying an existing business, the purchase agreement also becomes part of the core package because it defines the use of proceeds and the source of repayment.

SBA 7(a) documents

SBA 7(a) loans add SBA forms to the core package, plus program-specific supporting documents. SBA Form 1919 is required for every 7(a) loan; your lender will tell you which of the others apply to your deal, because SBA has changed these requirements in recent years.

  • SBA Form 1919 — Borrower Information Form. This is the primary application form. It captures the borrower's legal name, address, EIN, structure, purpose of the loan, and use-of-proceeds details.
  • SBA Form 912 — Statement of Personal History. Asks about prior criminal history, arrests, and delinquency on federal debt. Your lender will confirm which owners must complete it under current SBA rules.
  • SBA Form 413 — Personal Financial Statement. Commonly required from owners with a significant ownership share. It is a detailed balance sheet of personal assets, liabilities, and income.
  • SBA Form 159 — Fee Disclosure and Compensation Agreement. Required only when a broker, packager, or other consultant is being paid to help obtain the loan. It discloses any referral fees; SBA limits what agents may charge.

In addition to the forms above, plan to provide use-of-proceeds documentation: a purchase agreement or contract if you are buying equipment or real estate, quotes or invoices from vendors, and a payoff statement or written confirmation from any lender you are refinancing. The amount of detail depends on the size of the loan and the complexity of the transaction.

SBA 504 documents

SBA 504 loans finance fixed assets — real estate, machinery, and major equipment — so the documentation focuses on the project itself. The lender orders many of these items, but you will still need to supply supporting data.

  • Project cost breakdown. Itemized construction, equipment, and soft-cost figures.
  • Contractor bids or a signed construction contract. At least one formal bid from a licensed contractor, or the executed contract if work has already begun.
  • Environmental Phase I report. Required for real estate projects to identify contamination risks. The lender typically orders this, but you will need to provide access.
  • Real estate appraisal. Ordered by the lender or the Certified Development Company; you supply the property address and access.
  • Purchase agreement or existing mortgage statements. If you are buying, the contract. If you are refinancing existing debt, the current loan documents.
  • Equipment quotes. Quotes with vendor details, model numbers, and expected useful life of ten years or more.
  • Business plan with job-creation estimates. The 504 program requires a projection of how many full-time equivalent jobs the project will create or retain.

Microloan documents

SBA microloans, which are issued by nonprofit intermediaries rather than the SBA directly, generally ask for less than a 7(a) but more than a simple cash advance. Prepare the core package plus a detailed business plan, a collateral description, and recent personal financial statements. Microlenders also review your credit history more closely because the loan amounts are smaller and the underwriting standards vary by intermediary. The SBA publishes a list of approved microlenders at sba.gov/loans/microloans/list-of-microlenders.

Conventional commercial loans

Conventional commercial loans follow a shorter but sharper documentation path. Expect two to three years of business tax returns, current year-to-date financials, and rent rolls or lease abstracts if the collateral is income-producing real estate. Underwriters calculate the debt service coverage ratio — DSCR — to confirm the property generates enough income to cover the new debt. Most lenders look for a DSCR of 1.25x or better as a planning threshold; you can run those numbers yourself with the SBA and commercial calculators or review the methodology on How our numbers work.

Business line of credit

A revolving line of credit is secured by assets like receivables or inventory, so the emphasis is on cash-flow velocity rather than long-term fixed-asset value. Lenders typically ask for six to twelve months of business bank statements, an accounts receivable aging report, an accounts payable aging report, the latest debt schedule, and interim financial statements. Because the line is drawn and repaid on a rolling basis, the underwriter cares more about monthly cash flow consistency than annual profitability.

Residential mortgage documents

Residential mortgages have a more rigid, standardized list than most business loans because the files are sold to Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, or private investors. The core stack includes:

  • Thirty days of recent pay stubs.
  • Two years of W-2 forms from every wage-earning borrower.
  • Two years of federal tax returns with all schedules, including Schedule E for rental properties.
  • Two months of personal bank statements covering all deposit accounts.
  • Government-issued photo ID.
  • Executed purchase contract if you are buying, or the current mortgage statement if you are refinancing.
  • Gift letter if any portion of the down payment is coming from a family member or other donor. The letter must name the donor, the amount, state that no repayment is expected, and include the donor's bank account information.
  • Divorce decree, separation agreement, or child-support order if either borrower has ongoing obligations that affect debt-to-income.
  • HOA documentation, budget, and reserve study if the property is a condominium.

Self-employed borrowers add two years of personal returns plus a year-to-date profit and loss statement prepared by a CPA. The lender may also request a verification of employment for any co-borrower whose income is not wage-based.

How to organize the file

The way you name and sequence documents matters as much as the documents themselves. Underwriters review dozens of files per week; clean organization speeds the review and reduces the chance that a missing page holds up your closing.

Naming convention. Use a consistent pattern such as YYYY-MM description.pdf. For example, a January 2026 bank statement becomes 2026-01 bank-statement.pdf. This sorts chronologically and makes it easy to spot gaps.

PDF preferred. Submit documents as PDFs rather than Word documents, photos, or ZIP archives. PDFs print the same way on every computer, preserve formatting, and cannot be altered without leaving a digital trace. Certified copies are sometimes required at closing; ask your lender which documents need originals or certification.

Cover sheet. Place a one-page cover sheet at the front of each major section listing what is inside. A table of contents at the very front helps the underwriter navigate the packet.

One source of truth. Keep every document in a single master folder, then copy only the exact pages requested into the submission package. Do not send superseded versions, draft returns, or unsigned agreements.

What underwriters red-flag. Gaps in tax-filing history raise questions. Unexplained large deposits into business or personal accounts must be documented with a letter of explanation. Mixed personal and business accounts make it harder to verify revenue. Late filings — extensions, amended returns, or unfiled periods — weaken the file unless you explain them in writing.

FAQ

How far back do lenders want tax returns?

For most business loans, three full years. For residential mortgages, two years is the standard. If you file an extension, include the extension confirmation and the completed return once filed.

Can I use digital copies?

Almost always yes for the application and underwriting stage. Lenders prefer PDFs sent through a secure portal. At closing, some documents must be original or certified copies. Ask your lender early which items require hard originals.

What if I am self-employed?

Self-employed borrowers provide Schedule C or Schedule E from their personal return, plus any 1099s or K-1s that supplement income. Lenders look at a longer history — usually two to three years — to smooth out the year-to-year swings common in sole proprietorships and gig work.

How long should my bank statements be?

Twelve months for business loans, two months for residential mortgages. If the lender asks for more to explain a pattern, provide exactly the months requested rather than guessing.

Do all owners have to sign?

Every owner with 20 percent or more typically signs the loan documents and submits personal financial statements. Some lenders lower that threshold; none raise it. If an owner is between 10 and 19 percent, ask the lender whether a personal guarantee is still required.

How long does the whole process take?

For an SBA 7(a) loan, the timeline from completed application to funding commonly runs several weeks to a few months. Conventional commercial loans can be faster — two to eight weeks if the file is clean. Residential mortgages typically take 30 to 45 days from contract to close.

Do I need a business plan?

For startup loans, SBA 504 loans, and most microloans, yes. For established businesses with years of profitable tax returns, a concise overview is usually enough instead of a full plan.

What if I am missing a document?

Submit a letter of explanation and a timeline for when the missing item will be delivered. Lenders prefer transparency to silence, and most will hold the file open for a reasonable period rather than deny it outright.
