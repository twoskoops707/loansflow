How to Choose a Lender (or Broker)

Not all lenders are the same, and the lowest headline rate is rarely the cheapest loan. The lender you choose affects how quickly you close, what fees you pay, whether you can negotiate covenants, and whether the deal survives a second look from an underwriter. This guide covers the terms and trade-offs worth comparing before you sign anything.

APR vs. Interest Rate

The interest rate is the percentage applied to your outstanding balance each month. The Annual Percentage Rate (APR) bundles that rate with certain lender fees and closing costs, spread over the loan term, so it is usually slightly higher than the quoted rate. APR is meant to be an apples-to-apples comparison between two offers, but only if the loans are the same type and term. Always ask which fees are included in the APR figure, because origination points, underwriting fees, and SBA guaranty fees are sometimes excluded.

Fees to Compare

Loan estimates list fees in sections that do not always map cleanly to what you will actually pay. Pay attention to three kinds of fees. Origination fees are a flat dollar amount or a percentage of the loan charged for processing. SBA guarantee fees on 7(a) and 504 loans are calculated on the guaranteed portion, not the full amount. Prepayment penalties charge you for paying the loan off early or refinancing. Prepayment penalties are more common on conventional commercial loans and some SBA 504 deals than on 7(a) loans, but read the promissory note before you sign.

Term Length

A longer term lowers the monthly payment but increases total interest paid. For working capital or equipment, shorter terms often make sense because the asset depreciates faster than the loan matures. For commercial real estate, 20- to 25-year amortizations are common because the building is expected to hold value. The best term depends on the use of proceeds, not just the monthly payment you can afford.

Collateral and Personal Guarantees

SBA 7(a) and 504 loans almost always require collateral up to the loan amount, plus a personal guarantee from any owner with 20% or more ownership. Conventional commercial lenders often go further: they may lien business assets, accounts receivable, and inventory, and require a cross-collateralized personal guarantee that covers all business debts. The more collateral a lender requires, the more you have at risk if the business stalls.

Covenants and Reporting

Covenants are promises you make in the loan agreement. Affirmative covenants require you to do things — maintain insurance, submit financial statements, keep a minimum DSCR. Negative covenants restrict you from taking on more debt, selling collateral, or changing the business without the lender's consent. Breaching a covenant can trigger a default even if payments are current. Ask for a draft covenant package before you apply so you can see how onerous the reporting burden is.

Speed to Funding

SBA loans routinely take four to eight weeks from application to closing, sometimes longer during peak periods or if the file needs SBA central review. Conventional commercial loans can close in two to four weeks. SBA Preferred Lenders have delegated authority to approve certain loans without SBA central review, which can cut weeks off the timeline. If you need capital fast, ask the lender for their average close time on deals similar to yours.

SBA Preferred Lender Status

Preferred Lenders are banks and nonbank lenders the SBA has authorized to make credit decisions on eligible 7(a) loans using delegated authority, without waiting for SBA to review each file. The status does not mean better rates, but it usually means faster underwriting and a lender that knows the SBA program deeply. Ask whether the lender is a Preferred Lender, and if so, what their approval limit is for non-delegated authority.

Bank vs. Credit Union vs. Nonbank vs. Broker

Banks offer relationship pricing and often the lowest rates for borrowers with strong deposits and credit, but underwriting is slower and documentation heavier. Credit unions can be more flexible on credit and charge lower fees, but they are not available to every borrower and may have smaller lending limits. Nonbank lenders — online direct lenders and marketplace-backed programs — often approve faster and accept thinner files, but their rates can run higher than a bank's because they fund loans from their own capital or warehouse lines rather than deposits. Brokers and marketplaces do not lend money themselves; they shop your file to multiple lenders and charge a fee or receive a commission from the lender. The right choice depends on how fast you need the money, how complex your file is, and whether you value relationship pricing over speed.

Broker Compensation and Licensing

If you use a mortgage broker or an SBA loan broker, the broker must disclose compensation. On SBA loans, this disclosure happens through SBA Form 159, which the broker provides to you before you sign a fee agreement. Brokers are typically paid by the lender, not by you, but the arrangement should be explicit. You can verify whether a broker is licensed through the NMLS Consumer Access website (nmlsconsumeraccess.org), which lists state licenses, registrations, and any disclosed disciplinary history for mortgage and consumer finance professionals.

Red Flags

Upfront fees before a commitment. Legitimate lenders usually charge application and underwriting fees after you have agreed to terms, not before they have reviewed your file. Be suspicious of any fee paid before a commitment letter.

Guaranteed approval. No lender can guarantee approval before underwriting. If someone promises approval regardless of credit, cash flow, or collateral, walk away.

Pressure tactics. If a lender tells you the rate expires in hours, rushes you to sign, or refuses to give you the full promissory note and fee schedule for review, that is a warning sign. Take the document home, review it with counsel or a CPA, and compare it against at least one other offer.

Questions to Ask a Lender

  1. What is the all-in APR, and which fees are included or excluded from that figure?
  2. Can you provide the full fee schedule, including origination, underwriting, SBA guaranty, and wire fees?
  3. Is there a prepayment penalty, and if so, how is it calculated and over what period?
  4. What collateral and personal guarantee are required?
  5. What covenants or financial covenants will I be required to maintain?
  6. How often and in what format do you require financial reporting?
  7. What is your average close time for deals of this size and type?
  8. Are you an SBA Preferred Lender, and what is your non-delegated approval limit?
  9. Do you sell loans into the secondary market, and if so, what are the transfer terms?
  10. What happens if I miss a payment or breach a covenant — what is the cure period?
