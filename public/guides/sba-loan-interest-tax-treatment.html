Tax Treatment of SBA Loan Interest and Fees

Short answer: Interest on an SBA loan used for business purposes is generally deductible as a business expense, subject to limits such as Section 163(j). Loan principal is not deductible, and the SBA guarantee fee and other costs of getting the loan are generally deducted over the life of the loan rather than all in the year you pay them. Confirm your situation with your own CPA.

This guide is written for owners who are about to take an SBA loan and want to know how it shows up on the return. It covers general federal rules as of November 11, 2026; state rules, entity type and your facts can change the answer.

1. Interest: generally deductible

Interest you pay on a loan used in your trade or business is generally deductible as a business expense under Internal Revenue Code Section 163; IRS Publication 535 explains business interest. The loan proceeds are not income, and repaying principal is not a deduction. Only the interest, and the cost of obtaining the loan, flow through the profit and loss statement.

2. The Section 163(j) limit and the small-business exemption

Section 163(j) caps the business interest a taxpayer can deduct in a year. Many small businesses are exempt: a business generally qualifies if its average annual gross receipts for the three prior tax years are at or below an inflation-adjusted threshold, which the IRS lists as $32 million for 2026 (Rev. Proc. 2025-32) (IRS questions and answers on the business interest limitation). If you are under it, you do not compute the limit. If you are over it, you file Form 8990 (instructions) and any disallowed interest carries forward. Gross receipts of related businesses can be aggregated, so check the group, not only the one entity that signs the note.

3. The guarantee fee and closing costs are usually amortized

The SBA upfront guarantee fee, the lender's fees and other costs of obtaining the loan are generally treated as debt issuance costs, which are deducted over the term of the loan under Treasury Regulation 1.446-5 rather than in the year paid. This is true even if the fee is financed into the loan. If the loan is repaid early, the unamortized balance is generally deductible at that point. Some costs, such as appraisal fees tied to buying property, may instead be added to the property’s cost basis; ask your CPA to classify each line on the closing statement.

4. A worked example

Take the $350,000, 10-year, 10.00% example. The fee is $7,875 (3% of the $262,500 guaranteed portion). In year one, about $34,034 of the payments is interest. If the fee is amortized over 10 years it adds approximately $788 a year (the regulation uses a constant-yield method, so the exact amounts differ) of deduction, so the year-one financing cost on the return is roughly $34,822. At an illustrative 24% combined federal rate for a pass-through owner, that is about $8,357 of tax reduction; your rate will differ. The point for planning: the deduction is smaller than the fee you paid in cash at closing, and it is largest in the early years because interest is front-loaded (see the amortization table in the SBA 7(a) calculator).

5. Mixed-use proceeds and tracing

Interest is generally classified by how the proceeds are spent, not by what secures the loan (Treas. Reg. 1.163-8T). Proceeds used for business operations support a business-interest deduction; proceeds used for personal purposes do not. Interest on money used to build or produce property may have to be capitalized instead of deducted. Deposit loan proceeds into a business account, pay vendors from it, and keep a simple use-of-proceeds schedule, especially for a business purchase where part of the money pays the seller and part funds working capital.

6. S corporations, partnerships and personal guarantees

  • S corporations: a loan made to the corporation produces interest at the corporation level, which flows to shareholders on the K-1. A personal guarantee of the corporation’s loan does not, by itself, increase a shareholder’s stock basis; basis generally increases only if the shareholder actually pays on the guarantee or lends money directly. Loans the owner makes to the company are tracked separately. See the IRS page on S corporations.
  • Partnerships and LLCs: debt allocations affect partners’ basis and at-risk amounts; recourse and nonrecourse treatment matters.
  • Sole proprietors: interest goes on Schedule C if the loan is used in the business.
  • Reasonable compensation: loan proceeds do not fix a low-salary problem. S corporation owner-employees must be paid reasonable compensation, which also drives the cash flow lenders see.

7. Multi-state and credits

States do not always follow the federal interest limitation or the amortization timing, so a multi-state business should check each state’s conformity. Tax credits, including research credits, reduce tax, not operating cash flow, so lenders generally still evaluate repayment from pre-tax earnings; they do not change the DSCR math in DSCR from a CPA’s view.

8. What to bring to your CPA

  • The final closing statement with every fee, and which fees were financed
  • The note and the amortization schedule (calculator)
  • Your annual interest statement; ask the lender if one is not issued automatically
  • A use-of-proceeds schedule with dates and amounts
  • Related-party gross receipts, if you may be near the $32 million line

9. Checklist before you sign

  1. Ask your CPA which entity should borrow and who should guarantee.
  2. Ask the lender whether the fee will be paid in cash or financed.
  3. Open a dedicated account for proceeds.
  4. Ask your CPA to set up fee amortization in your books at closing, not at year end.
  5. Compare the after-tax cost of the loan, not only the rate, when you compare loan options.

General information, not tax advice. Tax law changes and depends on your facts; consult a CPA or tax professional before you act. Related: owner add-backs and EBITDA normalization, SBA 7(a) step by step.

Common questions

Is SBA loan interest tax deductible?

Interest on a loan used for business purposes is generally deductible, subject to limits such as Section 163(j). Interest on proceeds used for personal purposes is not.

Can I deduct the SBA guarantee fee in the year I pay it?

Generally no. It is usually treated as a debt issuance cost and deducted over the life of the loan, even if it is financed into the loan.

Does the Section 163(j) limit apply to my small business?

Many small businesses are exempt if average annual gross receipts for the prior three years are at or below the inflation-adjusted threshold, which the IRS lists as $32 million for 2026. Related businesses can be aggregated.

Does my personal guarantee increase my S corporation basis?

Generally no. A guarantee alone does not increase stock basis; paying on the guarantee or lending money directly to the company can.

Where do I report loan interest?

On the return that matches the borrowing entity: Schedule C for a sole proprietor, Form 1120-S for an S corporation, Form 1065 for a partnership.
