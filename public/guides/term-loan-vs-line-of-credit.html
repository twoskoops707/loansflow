Term Loan vs. Business Line of Credit: How to Choose

Short answer: Use a term loan for a one-time purchase with a known cost, such as equipment or an acquisition, and a line of credit for recurring or uneven working-capital needs, since you pay interest only on what you draw.

Business owners often ask which is "better," a term loan or a line of credit. The honest answer is that they solve different problems. The right choice depends on what you are paying for and when the money comes back. Here is how each works and how to match them to your situation.

How a term loan works

A term loan delivers a lump sum up front, which you repay in fixed installments over a set period, typically one to ten years for equipment and working capital, and longer for real estate. The rate may be fixed or variable. You pay interest on the full balance from day one, and each payment reduces the principal on a set schedule.

Good for: a one-time, identifiable investment such as equipment, a vehicle, a buildout, or an acquisition. Pay for the asset over about its useful life.

How a line of credit works

A line of credit gives you an approved limit you can draw on, repay, and draw on again. You pay interest only on the amount outstanding, and you may owe a small annual or draw fee. Lines often have shorter commitment periods and are renewed periodically. Some require a "clean-up" period each year in which the balance is brought to zero.

Good for: uneven cash flow, covering payroll while waiting for receivables, buying inventory ahead of a season, or having a safety net.

The cost comparison

Consider two ways to finance a $150,000 equipment purchase at an illustrative 10.5 percent rate:

Term loan examples, $150,000 at 10.5%
TermMonthly paymentTotal interest
5 years$3,224$43,445
7 years$2,529$62,444

The longer term saves nearly $700 a month but costs about $19,000 more in interest. That trade-off, cash flow today against total cost, is the central decision in any term loan.

A line behaves differently. Say you draw $40,000 at 12 percent for six months to cover a seasonal inventory build, then repay it. Simple interest is roughly $40,000 × 12% × 0.5 = $2,400. You only paid for the money while you used it. A term loan for the same $40,000 would charge interest on the whole balance for the full term whether or not you needed it all.

Lines usually carry higher rates than secured term loans, and variable rates can rise. Always ask for the all-in cost with fees. Our business line of credit calculator estimates the interest on the average balance you draw.

Collateral and guarantees

  • Term loans for equipment are often secured by the equipment itself, which can keep other assets free.
  • Lines are commonly secured by receivables, inventory, or a blanket lien on business assets, and almost always include a personal guarantee for small businesses.
  • Both can be SBA-backed. A 7(a) provides term financing, while CAPLines provides revolving working capital, as covered in our guide to smaller SBA options.

A simple rule for matching

  1. Will the asset last for years? Use a term loan sized to the asset's useful life.
  2. Is the need temporary or recurring? Use a line, and repay it from the cash cycle it supports.
  3. Are you unsure of the exact amount? A line's flexibility is worth a higher rate.
  4. Is the rate or monthly payment predictability more important? Fixed-rate term debt wins.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Financing long-term assets with a short-term line. If the line is not renewed, you can be forced to repay something you cannot.
  • Using a term loan for a seasonal gap. You pay interest on money you only needed for a few months.
  • Ignoring fees. Origination, unused-line, and renewal fees can change which option is cheaper.
  • Maxing the line. Many lenders reduce or freeze lines when balances stay near the limit.

Many healthy businesses use both: a term loan for fixed assets and a modest line as a buffer. Lenders will review your debt service coverage either way, so run that number before you apply.
