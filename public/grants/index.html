Search federal grants

This searches the official Grants.gov database live. Nothing here is a loan, and legitimate federal grants never charge a fee to apply.

Reality check before you search

SBA states that it does not provide grants to start or expand a business. Its grants go to nonprofits, universities and resource partners for counseling and training, plus narrow programs for research, manufacturing and exporting. Most federal money for businesses is in the form of loans.

Live counts appear here when the search is available.

That is why the best use of this page is finding grants that match a specific mission: research, agriculture, energy, rural development, community or nonprofit work. For general business funding, see the loan types and uncommon ways to finance.

How to read a grant listing

  • Eligibility matters most. Read the full listing on Grants.gov to confirm your type of organization qualifies.
  • Many grants reimburse costs after you spend the money, or require matching funds.
  • Deadlines are firm. Registration (including SAM.gov for most federal awards) can take weeks, so start early.
  • Ask for help. Your local SBDC or a university research office can review an application at no cost.

Programs small businesses should know about

Grants for operating or starting an ordinary business are rare, but several real programs exist. Which one fits depends on what your business does.

  • SBIR and STTR. Federal agencies with large research budgets set aside money for small businesses to develop new technology. Awards come in phases and require a research proposal. Details and open topics are at SBIR.gov.
  • Export help. SBA funds programs, run through state agencies, that help small businesses begin or grow exporting, such as trade shows and export training. Start with SBA’s grants page, which also explains what SBA does and does not fund.
  • State and local economic development. Many states, counties and cities offer small grants or matching funds for hiring, facade improvements, equipment, energy upgrades or opening in a targeted area. These are not on Grants.gov, so search your state’s economic development agency and local chamber of commerce.
  • Industry and company programs. Some corporations, foundations and trade associations run small-business grant competitions. Read the rules and eligibility closely, and be cautious about any entry fee.

What applying involves

  1. Register early. Most federal awards require a SAM.gov registration, which can take weeks to approve, and an account on Grants.gov.
  2. Read the full notice. The listing names who can apply, what costs are covered, any matching requirement and the deadline. Missing one requirement usually ends the application.
  3. Prepare the narrative and budget. Reviewers score a clear problem statement, a realistic plan, a budget tied to the plan and your ability to carry it out.
  4. Plan for reimbursement. Many grants pay back costs after you spend the money, so you may need cash or a line of credit first. See business lines of credit.
  5. Keep records. Awards come with reporting and audit rules. Your CPA can help set up the tracking from day one.

If your business does not fit a grant, the better route is usually a loan. Compare the options on the loan types page and the comparison pages.

Avoid grant scams

  • Anyone who calls, texts or messages you to say you won or are guaranteed a "government grant" is running a scam. Agencies do not cold-call people with grants.
  • You never pay a fee to apply on Grants.gov or to receive an award. Be wary of "grant coaching" that charges thousands of dollars for public information.
  • SBA says it communicates only from email addresses ending in @sba.gov.
  • Be cautious of upfront fees for a loan, "guaranteed approval," and pressure to sign today.
