Terms used in this calculator

Owner pay
The salary the owner takes from the business. In seller-discretionary-earnings it is added back; in a global DSCR it is counted as personal income because it pays the owner’s household.
Interest expense
Interest the business paid on its existing debt. It is added back to profit when measuring cash flow available for debt, because loan payments are counted separately.
Depreciation and amortization (D&A)
Accounting costs that spread the price of equipment or other long-lived assets over several years. They lower reported profit but are not cash leaving the business, so lenders usually add them back when measuring cash flow.
Add-backs
Expenses that lowered reported profit but will not continue, or are personal to the owner, such as a one-time legal bill or a personal vehicle. Lenders and buyers accept only add-backs you can document.
Debt service
The total principal and interest you pay on loans in a year. It is the figure lenders set your cash flow against. Include the new loan and any loans you already have.
Owner pay
The salary the owner takes from the business. In seller-discretionary-earnings it is added back; in a global DSCR it is counted as personal income because it pays the owner’s household.
Other personal income
Income from outside the business, such as a spouse’s wages, rent or investment income, if you can document it.
Personal income taxes
The income taxes you pay personally on the business and other income. A global cash-flow analysis subtracts them before measuring coverage.
Living expenses
Your household’s everyday costs. Lenders subtract an amount for them in a global cash-flow analysis because that money is not available for loan payments.
Monthly debt payments
The minimum payments you owe each period on loans and cards, such as a car loan, student loan or mortgage. Lenders count them when judging how much more debt you can carry.

How to use it

Start with the business: net income after owner pay, interest, depreciation and amortization, and any add-backs you can document. Then enter the business debt payments, including the new loan. For the global view, add the owner pay taken from the business, other personal income, personal taxes, living expenses and personal debt payments.

How it is calculated

Business cash flow = net income + interest + depreciation and amortization + add-backs. Business DSCR = business cash flow ÷ business debt service. Global cash flow = business cash flow + owner pay + other personal income − personal income taxes − living expenses. Global DSCR = global cash flow ÷ (business debt service + personal debt payments). The global view matters for owner-operated businesses because the owner’s household has to live on the same cash.

Worked example

With net income of $150,000, interest of $20,000, depreciation of $25,000 and $15,000 of add-backs, business cash flow is $210,000. Against $110,000 of business debt service the business DSCR is 1.91x. Adding $90,000 of owner pay and $40,000 of other income, then subtracting $30,000 of taxes and $60,000 of living expenses, global cash flow is $250,000. With $24,000 of personal debt, total debt service is $134,000 and the global DSCR is 1.87x.

How to read the result

Many lenders look for at least 1.25x. Add-backs only count when you can document them, and lenders differ on how they treat taxes and living expenses, so treat this as a planning tool. For the reasoning behind each adjustment read DSCR from a CPA’s view and add-backs and EBITDA.

Common questions

What is the difference between business and global DSCR?

Business DSCR looks only at the company. Global DSCR combines the company and the owner, including personal income, taxes, living costs and personal debt.

Which add-backs are accepted?

Documented, non-recurring or personal items. Unsupported add-backs are usually rejected.

What global DSCR do lenders want?

It varies, but many look for about 1.25x or more.
